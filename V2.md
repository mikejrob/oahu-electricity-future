# Planned second edition (v2): from a single-node to a regional model

The current analysis models Oʻahu as a single zone: one bus, no transmission
constraints, no locational value. That is the standard first approximation in
capacity-expansion work, and its main results are robust to it — but the next
round of questions is locational, and v2 is being built to answer them.

## The centerpiece: a nodal model of the Oʻahu grid

A multi-zone (regional/nodal) representation of the island's transmission
system, designed to answer:

- **What transmission does the utility-scale buildout actually require?**
  Solar concentrated in a few regions must move to load centers; the wires
  cost of every pathway is currently unmodeled, for all pathways alike.
- **What is distributed generation and storage really worth?** Rooftop and
  canopy solar, and batteries at the point of consumption, can defer
  transmission and distribution upgrades. The single-node model credits none
  of that — its distributed-solar results are a floor. The nodal model prices
  the offset directly.
- **Where should renewable-energy zones be?** Siting, hosting capacity, and
  interconnection queues become analyzable when the grid has geography.

## Foundations already built

Two companion efforts feed the nodal model directly: the public land study
([solar-wind-landuse](https://github.com/mikejrob/solar-wind-landuse) — cap
counterfactuals, ownership × grid-proximity joins, corridor candidates, and
the legislative record of the land-use rules) and the grid-modeling
repository (`oahu-grid`, private during development — interface graph, zonal
loads, DER geography, recovered solar-cluster coordinates). Early scoping
from these: ~70 percent of the screened utility-solar resource lies north of
the island's transmission necks, and the corridor upgrades to move it south
are bounded at roughly $10–200 million — the central quantity the nodal
model will price properly.

## Also queued for v2

- **Land screening at finer resolution** — the Flat/Moderate/Steep slope
  tiers (already on all reference-land scenarios) extended to the
  land-constrained inventory; the gradient refined to 5-percentage-point
  slope bins; the agrivoltaic-eligible pool characterized; an
  excluded-lands inventory (federal landholdings, urban-district disturbed
  lands, closed golf acreage, reservoir surfaces) built out in the
  companion land repo.
- **Onshore wind re-screened under current Honolulu rules** — the model
  caps onshore wind at 150 MW total (existing plants plus modest headroom),
  a blunt stand-in for the county's setback restrictions, and every solved
  scenario builds to that cap. v2 replaces it with a parcel-level screen
  under the current ordinance, parallel to the solar screens.
- **Multi-day and climate-stress reliability** — chronological
  production-cost simulation beyond the single historical worst day.
- **Real-time pricing and inter-day storage** — both currently excluded,
  both favoring the renewable-heavy paths.
- **Rooftop solar measured, not inferred** — the single largest measurement
  ambiguity left in v1, and one that code review cannot settle.

  Three public series count Oʻahu's rooftop fleet and none states its
  rating basis. The permit-and-interconnection record behind this report
  reaches 793 MW by mid-2025; Hawaiian Electric's quarterly data reports
  79,347 Oʻahu systems without a stated basis; EIA's monthly utility data,
  which is AC, shows about 645 MW. System counts agree across the sources
  to within half a percent, and the capacity gaps match a typical
  DC-to-AC inverter loading ratio (about 1.14), so the likely reading is
  that the utility and permit series are DC nameplate while EIA is AC —
  but no source says so (Appendix A.11).

  Two things follow. First, comparisons with outside figures are unsafe
  without stating the basis on both sides. Second, and more consequential
  for the model, the netting chain has to be consistent end to end: the
  trajectories are calibrated in reported megawatts (A.11), then netted
  from load at the model's own site-level capacity factors (A.12), which
  come from a separate pipeline. If those capacity factors and the
  trajectory megawatts sit on different bases, distributed energy is
  mis-scaled in every scenario by roughly the loading ratio — on the order
  of 10 percent. v1's estimates are internally consistent by construction,
  because every coefficient is estimated per reported megawatt on the same
  series used to project net load, but "internally consistent" is a weaker
  claim than "measured," and the difference is worth closing.

  v2 closes it empirically rather than by inspection: measuring installed
  panel area directly from aerial and street-level imagery, converted to
  capacity with panel-efficiency vintages, to produce an independent
  estimate of the fleet on a stated basis. That also yields the rooftop
  *potential* derivation this report currently carries from an OSM
  roof-area screen it cannot vendor, and it re-anchors all three adoption
  trajectories to a measured stock.

  Why this waits for v2 rather than delaying v1: the trajectories' 2027
  anchor (800 MW) already sits within one percent of the observed
  mid-2025 stock, so re-anchoring alone would move little; the growth-rate
  uncertainty that does matter is bracketed by running three trajectories
  (1,000 / 1,560 / 2,120 MW by 2050) whose system costs span about $1.9
  billion, all reported throughout; and the direction of effect is known
  and stated — more rooftop lowers system cost in every scenario while
  differences between scenarios move little. Resolving the measurement
  properly needs fieldwork-grade data, not a re-solve.
- **Completed vendoring** — the EGS resource screen, rooftop-potential
  derivation, PSIP-era conversion capital, contract-structure documentation.
- Conversion cases with restored conversion capital.
